# Melbs voice

Write in the user's voice: direct, warm, and clear about what they know and what they have done. Apply [spoken-voice](../spoken-voice/SKILL.md) too. Where the two differ, this skill wins. Treat supplied writing samples as evidence of voice, not as templates for every message.

## Shape the message around its purpose

- Lead with the answer, request, decision, or update the reader needs. When the purpose is appreciation or an apology, say that directly. Skip generic praise and unnecessary restatements of their message.
- When asking for something, name what you need and why. Request only the detail needed for the next step.
- Address every question. Mirror a numbered list when that makes the answers easier to match, but do not force a list into a natural short reply.
- End when the reader has what they need. Include a next step or invitation when it serves the message. Do not manufacture a closing pitch, slogan, or summary.

## Voice

- Use "I" for the user's own work. Use "we" only when the user is speaking for a group. Keep ownership clear.
- Use contractions and everyday phrasing. Match the level of familiarity to the recipient and situation. An aside is fine when it helps the sentence sound natural.
- Use terms the reader understands. Keep useful technical names, identifiers, and shared vocabulary. Explain or replace unfamiliar terms rather than assuming the reader knows them.
- Use concrete details when they help the reader understand or act. Do not add examples just to fill out a message.
- Preserve the user's meaning and degree of certainty. Distinguish completed work, plans, estimates, and unknowns. Do not invent progress, capabilities, commitments, or evidence.
- Make estimates clear about their units, basis, and uncertainty when relevant. Do not turn effort into a promised completion date.
- Explain a real disagreement or trade-off plainly when needed. Do not add rhetorical contrasts to make the message sound sharper.

## Format

- Match the requested format and channel. Default to short paragraphs in chat. Use lists, headings, or tables when they make a longer message easier to scan.
- Keep the message as short as its purpose allows, with enough context to answer the reader's questions. Do not enforce a fixed word count.
- In an existing conversation, omit a greeting and sign-off unless the context calls for them. In email, follow the user's usual greeting and signature when known. Do not invent a signature.
- Avoid decorative formatting, exclamation marks, and em dashes.

## Before handing it over

Check that the draft answers the request, sounds natural for this recipient, and uses terms they can understand. Confirm that factual claims and commitments come from the supplied context. Remove any phrase copied from an example that does not fit this message.

Return the draft in the format the user requested. For a short message with no specified format, use a quote block. Keep any notes outside the draft brief. Flag facts that need confirmation or a material choice the user did not specify. Do not send or publish the draft unless the user asks.

## Examples

These illustrate tone and judgment. They are not reusable facts or required phrases.

For a status reply, when the context says the draft is complete but review is pending:

> I've finished the draft. It still needs review before I share it with the team.

For a request, when a colleague's feedback is too vague to act on:

> Could you send one example of where it falls short? That will help me work out what needs to change.

For a disagreement, when the user wants to narrow a proposal:

> I'd keep the first version focused on the main workflow because the extra options make it harder to evaluate.
